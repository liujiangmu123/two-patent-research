From Point Clouds to FEM: A Practical Framework for  
the Automated Modeling of Transmission Towers

Abstract 
Finite element modeling is central to the safety assessment and structural health monitor-
ing of transmission towers, yet conventional workflows remain labor-intensive and de-
pend on complete engineering drawings, which are often unavailable for aging towers. 
This study proposes an automated finite element modeling framework based on un-
manned aerial vehicle (UAV) point clouds. Multi-view stereo reconstruction with geomet-
ric–photometric depth refinement is used to generate dense three-dimensional point 
clouds, from which key nodal coordinates are extracted and converted into executable 
ANSYS APDL command streams. Material properties, boundary conditions, and loads 
are then assigned automatically to construct computable finite element models. This 
method was first validated using concrete cubes and Q235B steel members and was fur-
ther evaluated on a scaled transmission tower through comparison with the commercial 
SmartTower workflow. The results show that the proposed approach reduces modeling 
time from 1.5 days to 4.3 h, corresponding to an 88% efficiency improvement, while keep-
ing nodal coordinate errors within 10% and modal frequency errors within 3.7%. The 
framework satisfies the basic requirements of transmission tower numerical analysis and 
provides a practical non-contact solution for the rapid assessment of aging towers lacking 
design documentation. 
Keywords: transmission tower; structural health monitoring; computer vision; point 
cloud model reconstruction; finite element modeling 
 
1. Introduction 
As critical infrastructure components of modern power grids, transmission towers 
play a vital role in ensuring reliable electricity transmission across long distances while 
withstanding extreme environmental loads [1]. Statistical reports indicate the length of 
220 kV+ voltage level transmission lines in China rising from 5.439 billion km to 9.197 
billion km between 2013 and 2023 (69% growth) [2]. The magnitude of this grid expansion 
proportionally increases the operational and maintenance burdens on infrastructure en-
gineers, particularly for aging tower networks. However, these towers are often located 
in remote and harsh environments, where they are susceptible to multiple environmental 
stressors, such as high winds, lightning strikes, ice loads, corrosion, and material degra-
dation caused by mechanical fatigue [3,4]. These factors can lead to the gradual accumu-

lation of structural damage, and if these damages are not detected and addressed 
promptly, they may reduce the load-bearing capacity of the tower and even lead to struc-

mission towers are especially crucial [5–8]. 
Traditional SHM for transmission towers predominantly employs a dual approach 
combining physical sensor networks and finite element analysis (FEA) [9]. Sensor deploy-
ment techniques involve placing sensors at critical points of the tower to collect real-time 
deformation and displacement responses, thereby reflecting the actual state of the struc-
ture and identifying localized issues. Meanwhile, the FEA of transmission towers can as-
sess the overall structural behavior. Ultimately, by integrating the strengths of both meth-
ods, the monitoring system is capable of performing comprehensive modal identification 
and reliability assessments of transmission towers. Nonetheless, for large structures such 
as transmission towers that frequently carry high-voltage electricity during operation, 
physical sensor networks face significant challenges including difficult installation, high 
costs, limited measurement points, and restricted monitoring durations [10]. More criti-
cally, FEA implementation encounters two fundamental limitations: (i) labor-intensive 
model construction requiring 5–7 days per tower—increasingly inadequate for growing 
inspection demands—and (ii) mandatory reliance on as-built drawings. Industry reports 
and our fieldwork confirm that a large number of the aging towers in the areas studied 
lack complete drawings. 
In recent years, with the rapid development of image processing technology, com-
puter vision-based monitoring methods have emerged as a promising alternative [11,12]. 
With advantages such as long-range capability, non-contact measurement, high precision, 
and time efficiency, these methods address many limitations of traditional approaches 
and have increasingly gained favor among engineers and researchers [13–17], gradually 
finding application in practical engineering projects. Computer vision-based monitoring 
systems typically incorporate techniques such as feature detection, feature extraction, fea-
ture matching, and feature tracking, analyzing digital image and video data to assess 
structural conditions [18]. And they have shown promising capabilities in areas such as 
displacement time-history signal acquisition [19–21], structural crack detection [22], and 
deformation damage assessment [23]. Tang et al. [24] proposed a method for the real-time, 
continuous monitoring of the structural posture of long-span bridge cables. The feasibility 
of this approach was verified through theoretical simulations, laboratory tests, and prac-
tical applications. Zhu et al. [25] proposed a dual-camera-based visual monitoring system 
to address displacement monitoring during the construction and post-construction phases 
of prefabricated subway stations, and this system has been implemented in real engineer-
ing projects. Zhu et al. [26] developed a computer vision-based structural vibration iden-
tification system using techniques such as image preprocessing, phase optical flow esti-
mation, mode decomposition, and phase motion amplification, successfully detecting 
structural vibrations in complex environments. Experimental and field test results indi-
cate that this system can accurately detect structural vibrations in complex environments. 
However, the overall technical approach of the visual monitoring systems developed 
by researchers has primarily focused on the collection of real-time monitoring data, with 
limited contribution to the establishment of structural mechanical models. For engineer-
ing structures such as transmission towers, comprehensive and real-time health monitor-
ing is essential, and FEM remains the standard method for addressing global analysis 
challenges. Therefore, exploring the integration of traditional FEM with computer vision-
based monitoring systems is of significant importance. 
Since the beginning of the 21st century, with the rapid advancement of computa-
tional capabilities, computer vision has achieved revolutionary progress, which also 
serves as an inspiration for the methods proposed in this paper. Researchers have started

integrating computer vision with machine learning, deep learning [27–29], UAVs [30], and 
other advanced algorithms to explore increasingly complex visual monitoring tasks, in-

and force of cables by extracting the coordinates of key cable regions, applying segmented 
chain theory and nonlinear optimization, and was validated on single-curvature cable net 
structures. Angelosanti et al. [40] introduced a new workflow that directly converts mag-

information modeling (BIM) systems. This approach addresses the challenge of efficiently 
managing and visualizing large-scale dynamic SHM data. 
To the best of the authors’ knowledge, existing visual monitoring systems primarily 
focus on improving the accuracy of real-time measurement data and the creation of virtual 
3D models, while relatively little research has been conducted on the integration of visual 
technologies with FEM. To overcome the limitations of traditional FEM methods, which 
are heavily reliant on engineering drawings, time-consuming, labor-intensive, and com-
plex to operate, this study proposes an automated FEM approach for transmission towers 
via UAV-Based Point Clouds. The proposed approach utilizes advanced multi-view vi-
sion principles to reconstruct the target structure’s point cloud model through UAV-cap-
tured multi-angle images, achieving over 95% feature coverage. Subsequently, depth-
aware densification through geometric photometric optimization yields a dense point 
cloud with substantially higher number of points and accuracy. Finally, based on the point 
cloud model, the automated APDL (ANSYS Parametric Design Language) command 
stream generator developed by the research team is used to systematically connect the key 
nodes and assign material properties, loads, and boundary conditions, which are then in-
tegrated into the ANSYS APDL command stream to achieve the automated finite element 
modeling of transmission towers. This approach reduces labor and resource consumption, 
significantly enhances modeling efficiency, and aims to establish an FEM using computer 
vision technology. Section 2 of this paper provides a detailed explanation of the principles 
of the proposed method, while Sections 3 and 4 experimentally verify the feasibility and 
applicability of the method. Section 5 provides a summary of the entire paper and offers 
an outlook on the future development of this field. 
2. Materials and Methods 
2.1. Basic Steps 
The FEM approach to the automation of transmission towers proposed in this study 
is centered on the point cloud model reconstruction technology. As shown in Figure 1, 
this method comprises three main components: the collection and processing of image 
datasets, sparse and dense point cloud reconstruction, and command stream generation 
for FEM.

Firstly, a UAV is used to capture images of the target structure from various angles, 
followed by image processing to obtain an unordered image dataset of the structure. Sub-
sequently, feature detection and matching are performed on the image dataset. Based on 
the registration results, triangulation, pose estimation, and model optimization are con-
ducted to generate a sparse point cloud model of the structure. Following this, based on 
multi-view stereo (MVS) principles, the depth information from the image dataset is uti-
lized to fill and fit the sparse model using optical and geometric consistency, thereby com-
pleting the dense point cloud reconstruction. Finally, the coordinates of the dense point 
cloud model are transformed into actual coordinates using the rigid body transformation 
law, which are then integrated with FEM software （ANSYS 18.0）for the FEM of the 
structure. This novel approach successfully converts virtual three-dimensional models es-
tablished through visual technology into mechanical models of engineering structures.

2.2. Image Acquisition and Processing 
The collection of image datasets is a critical stage in the research process, directly 
influencing the accuracy and quality of the final point cloud model. In this study, the orig-
inal image set consists of video frames captured by a camera mounted on a UAV. This 
method ensures that all details of the structure’s surface are captured, thereby enhancing 
the completeness and reliability of the point cloud model and significantly saving labor 
and resources. Additionally, to ensure the accuracy of the FEA of the final structure, it is 
essential to capture images with consistent camera parameters. Therefore, disabling fea-
tures such as autofocus, automatic FPS, and auto white balance during shooting is also an 
important step. 
Considering that reconstructing a point cloud model is often computationally inten-
sive, it is critical to maintain the coordinate accuracy of numerous point sets. This study

proposes that the careful processing of the original image set can potentially address this

tionally, in computer vision, lighting and noise often affect the accuracy of feature detec-
tion; thus, preprocessing operations such as noise reduction and contrast enhancement 
are necessary to optimize the image set. 
2.3. Point Cloud Model Reconstruction 
Point cloud model reconstruction is the most critical stage in performing the FEM of 
civil engineering structures using computer vision systems. This process utilizes the prin-
ciples of “pinhole imaging” and multi-view vision to convert real 3D coordinates to planar 
2D coordinates and recover the target structure in a computer-constructed virtual coordi-
nate system. After obtaining the image dataset, this stage primarily comprises sparse 
point cloud reconstruction, depth information acquisition, and dense point cloud recon-
struction. A schematic of the point cloud model reconstruction process is shown in Figure

2.3.1. Constructing Sparse Point Cloud Model of Transmission Tower 
In the sparse point cloud reconstruction module, the main approach adopted in this 
study is to incrementally add images for feature detection and matching, iteratively per-
forming the 3D reconstruction of the target structure. Simultaneously, the camera poses 
are estimated based on the reconstructed model, and a global optimization method is ap-
plied to continuously improve model accuracy until all usable images in the dataset are 
fitted. The final output includes all camera parameters and a sparse point cloud model 
that provides the basis for subsequent dense point cloud reconstruction. 
Given the “multi-corner, multi-edge” characteristics of transmission tower struc-
tures, this study selects the Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) algorithm for feature 
detection. This algorithm, with its robust scale and rotation invariance, is particularly 
suited for image matching and 3D reconstruction in computer vision applications. During 
feature detection and extraction, the computer identifies feature points of the transmission 
tower structure based on specified recognition parameters. To enhance the accuracy of 
point cloud reconstruction, appropriate peak and edge response thresholds must be ad-
justed, although this may impact computational speed. Additionally, the detected feature 
points include RGB values, which improve the realism of the point cloud model and fa-
cilitate the selection of key node locations for constructing the FEM. Moreover, EXIF files 
within the image set, containing the camera’s internal parameters such as focal length, 
aperture, and shutter speed, are also extracted to prepare for subsequent steps. 
Next, the detected and extracted feature points are matched. Given the large number 
of feature points, this study uses the “nearest neighbor search algorithm” to accelerate 
matching between image datasets. Symmetric matching and the RANSAC algorithm are 
applied to filter and optimize the matched pairs, effectively improving the accuracy and 
reliability of the matching process. Symmetric matching refers to bidirectional matching 
between two images: first matching from Image A to Image B and then from Image B to 
Image A. Only feature pairs that match successfully in both directions are retained. The 
retained matching pairs are then subjected to geometric verification to ensure they meet 
geometric constraints. After matching is completed, a feature point matching matrix is 
generated. The displayed image is a heatmap of the matching results, where colors range 
from dark (red) to light (blue), representing confidence levels from high to low. This 
heatmap reflects, to some extent, the density of feature matching between image sets as 
well as the accuracy of the point cloud model. 
In fact, the previous steps only completed the initial matching by selecting the high-
est-quality image pair from the dataset as a foundation, without utilizing the full infor-
mation from the entire dataset. To complete the initial sparse point cloud model, incre-
mental reconstruction is required. As shown in Figure 3, using the initial matched pair as 
the base, additional images are gradually added to the reconstruction process. For each 
new image added, feature matching and 3D point cloud updates are performed. Mean-
while, using the previously obtained internal camera parameters and the initial camera 
pose estimation, the Perspective-n-Point (PnP) algorithm is applied to estimate the camera 
pose of the new image. This is an iterative updating process, where the continuous addi-
tion of images and viewpoints iteratively improves and refines the sparse point cloud 
model. Unlike the previous feature matching validation process between two images, in-
cremental reconstruction requires multi-image matching validation with loop closure ver-
ification. This ensures that feature point matches in the image sequence form a consistent 
loop, thereby enhancing the reliability of the matches. 
Finally, this study further optimizes and refines the generated point cloud model. 
Through statistical and geometric validation, incorrectly matched feature points and mis-
estimated 3D points were removed, and the accuracy of the camera pose and 3D point

2.3.2. Getting Depth Information from Image Dataset 
The sparse point cloud model contains only a limited number of 3D points and may 
have considerable gaps and incomplete regions. To accurately describe the surface details 
of the structure and transition to a dense point cloud, depth information serves as a crucial 
bridge. Depth information here refers to the distance between each pixel and the camera. 
This study employs an MVS reconstruction approach, primarily involving disparity cal-
culation, matching cost computation for corresponding features on reference images, cost 
aggregation, and optimization to ultimately obtain depth information. However, before 
this process, the original image set must be undistorted, as skipping this step would in-
troduce significant errors. 
Initial depth estimation is first conducted using feature point matching results, ap-
plying geometric constraints and photometric consistency to obtain preliminary depth in-
formation. Geometric constraints ensure the spatial relationships of feature points across 
different viewpoints, while photometric consistency ensures that corresponding points 
have similar color or brightness. The basic principle of disparity calculation is used here 
to convert disparity into depth information. Disparity represents the positional difference 
in the same object across images from different viewpoints and is inversely related to 
depth information. The calculation process is shown in Equations (1) and (2):

Finally, the calculated depth information is optimized and refined to eliminate the 
effects of noise and discontinuities, resulting in the final depth information. The computer 
also integrates “photometric consistency” and “geometric consistency” to generate high-
precision depth maps and normal vector maps. For photometric consistency, the com-
puter uses lighting information from the image set (such as pixel color and brightness) to 
determine the correspondences of the same physical point from different perspectives. 
However, relying solely on photometric consistency is prone to errors due to variations 
in lighting, shadows, and reflections and a lack of texture. In contrast, geometric con-
sistency depends on the position and structure of points in 3D space and is unaffected by 
lighting, reflections, or texture, providing more robust matching. The complementary na-
ture of these two methods enables the computer to achieve optimal depth information. 
At this stage, by combining the detection of surface feature points from the sparse 
point cloud model with the acquisition of depth information for all pixel points in the 
image set, the conditions are met to construct a dense point cloud model that more closely 
represents the actual structure. 
2.3.3. Constructing Dense Point Cloud Model of Transmission Tower 
In order to construct a high-quality dense point cloud model, after the first few steps 
of obtaining depth information from the image dataset, the steps of depth map fusion, 
filling, and optimization are required to obtain the optimal solution of the fitted surface of 
the densely filled sparse point cloud. Depth map fusion involves aggregating depth maps 
from multiple viewpoints to generate a unified point set that represents the 3D structure 
of the entire scene. During the aggregation process, overlapping regions from multiple 
viewpoints are depth-fused, selecting the most reliable depth values to form an optimal 
point set that includes as much scene detail as possible. Next, based on the original sparse 
point cloud spatial coordinate system, the aggregated optimal point set is incorporated 
into the previously generated sparse point cloud model and corrected using an error cor-
rection algorithm. 
At this point, a dense point cloud model of the transmission tower structure is suc-
cessfully constructed. This virtual model will be used for the FEM of the engineering struc-
ture. 
2.4. Automated Finite Element Modeling Based on Point Cloud Models 
In civil engineering, the construction and analysis of FEM structures involve estab-
lishing a mechanical model and computing and integrating discrete elements. Taking a 
transmission tower as an example, engineers typically establish a mechanical model based 
on the actual structure of the tower and use modeling tools to create the corresponding 

Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 6640 
9 of 23 
 
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16136640 
structural model. They then use software like Abaqus 2023, ANSYS 18.0, or HyperMesh 
2022 to divide the computational object into multiple numbered elements for FEA. The 
novel FEM method proposed in this study significantly accelerates the process of con-
structing the mechanical model. The help of a dense point cloud model provides precise 
coordinate information on key transmission tower nodes. This study uses ANSYS FEA 
software (ANSYS 18.0) as an example and develops an automated APDL command 
stream generator. The developed algorithm takes nodal data extracted from the point 
cloud model as input and systematically connects these nodes to construct the structural 
framework. Crucially, the generator integrates a comprehensive material property data-
base—a 15-year curated repository by our research team—containing standardized pa-
rameters for transmission tower components, boundary conditions, and load configura-
tions [41–50]. This database enables rapid parameter assignment to the generated frame-
work, with the system ultimately exporting executable APDL commands in TXT format. 
It should be emphasized that material properties are not derived from the point cloud 
itself but are assigned from a predefined database of standard transmission tower mate-
rials and sections. The vision-based inference of mechanical properties remains beyond 
the scope of this study. 
To demonstrate the contribution of the proposed method to the mechanical modeling 
of transmission towers, this study adopted a two-phase validation approach. Initially, fea-
sibility assessment tests were conducted on fundamental concrete blocks and steel struc-
tural specimens to verify methodological validity. Subsequently, the developed FEM 
scripting system was applied to establish a scaled transmission tower model (Figure 4), 
where the automatically generated APDL commands were executed in ANSYS for nu-
merical simulation. Modal analysis served as the representative case study to evaluate 
modeling performance, with comprehensive experimental procedures and the analytical 
results detailed in Sections 3 and 4.

4. Field-Applicable Validation: Scaled Transmission Tower Modeling 
via UAV Photogrammetry 
4.1. Experimental Overview 
To verify the feasibility of the proposed innovative FEM method, this study con-
ducted an outdoor field test on a “transmission tower model.” The dimensions and struc-
ture of the transmission tower model are shown in Figure 8. In the experiment, FEMs of 
the target tower were constructed using both point cloud reconstruction technology and 
traditional engineering drawings. The natural frequencies and mode shapes of the struc-
ture were selected as FEA results for comparative analysis. 
Firstly, throughout the experiment, the main image acquisition device used was a DJI 
UAV, model Matrice M300 RTK (Da-Jiang Innovations, Shenzhen, China), equipped with a 
Saier 6100 gimbal camera and a 40MM professional surveying lens. The camera’s perfor-
mance was comparable to that of a standard smartphone, with the camera model 
SHARE6100PLUS061X, an aperture of f/0, ISO sensitivity of ISO-250, and a fixed focal 
length of 40 mm. The final resolution of the captured raw image data was 9504 × 6336 
pixels, and the EXIF file version for the automatic reading of camera internal parameters 
by the computer was 0231. In principle, the image dataset used for generating the point 
cloud model with computer vision technology needs to meet the following requirements: 
(1) capture a large number of photos from multiple angles; (2) ensure the lens captures the 
entire structure’s outline; (3) maintain clear and visible images; (4) ensure at least 50% 
overlap between images in the raw dataset to guarantee accuracy in subsequent feature 
matching as well as the continuity and completeness of the point cloud model; and (5)

4.2.3. Experimental Comparison of Finite Element Modeling Methods for Transmission 
Towers Based on Point Cloud Techniques and SmartTower Finite Element  
Modeling Methods 
Based on the generated high-precision point cloud model, the finite element model 
of the transmission tower was successfully constructed in this study using the aforemen-
tioned method. The node information after coordinate conversion was inputted into the 
developed automated APDL command flow generator, and the nodes were connected in 
an orderly manner to form the model framework. Appropriate material properties and 
boundary conditions were then assigned. Specifically, the model uses a material with an 
elastic modulus of 69 GPa, Poisson’s ratio of 0.3, and density of 3000 kg/m3—representa-
tive of an aluminum alloy or similar lightweight structural material. Three cross-sectional 
types were defined, corresponding to L-shaped angle sections of different dimensions: 
Section 1 (L40 × 40 × 3), Section 2 (L20 × 20 × 1), and Section 3 (L30 × 30 × 2). The finite 
element model employs BEAM188 and BEAM189 elements for discretization, with each 
structural member typically represented by a single element. Fixed boundary conditions 
are applied at four key support points (keypoints 7000, 7001, 7002, 7003) to simulate rigid 
supports. The final output is a complete APDL command flow that can be directly im-
ported into ANSYS software. The command stream can be analyzed by the finite element 
software for subsequent mechanical analysis, thus realizing the complete transformation 
from a 3D point cloud model to a computable mechanical model.

FEM system, a comparative analysis was conducted against SmartTower V2024—a com-

5. Conclusions 
This study presents an innovative framework for the automated finite element mod-
eling of transmission towers through UAV-based point cloud reconstruction. The key con-
tributions and findings can be summarized as follows: 
(1) Methodological Innovation: The proposed pipeline from computer vision to finite 
element modeling establishes an automated workflow that connects UAV photo-
grammetry (90% feature coverage) to structural analysis. Multi-view stereo match-
ing, geometric photometric refinement, and the development of an automated APDL 
command generator allow for the conversion of dense point clouds into finite ele-
ment models, filling an important research gap in non-contact structural modeling. 
It should be noted that research at this intersection is currently very limited, and di-
rect quantitative comparison with other methods is not yet feasible. 
(2) Technical Validation: This study systematically validated the effectiveness and feasi-
bility of the proposed method through benchmark tests on concrete specimens and 
steel structural components, as well as field tests on a scaled transmission tower 
model. The experimental results demonstrate that the method achieves high model-
ing accuracy for target structures. Compared with traditional FEM software (e.g., 
SmartTower), the proposed approach maintains nodal coordinate errors within 10%, 
limits frequency errors in modal analysis to 3.7%, and shows excellent consistency in 
mode shapes. Remarkably, it improves modeling efficiency by 88%, reducing the con-
ventional 1.5-day workflow to under 5 h. These results meet industry standards for 
civil engineering monitoring accuracy, confirming the reliability of the technical ap-
proach. 
(3) Engineering Significance: This approach solves two core pain points in transmission 
tower modeling through point cloud technology: first, it breaks through the depend-
ence of traditional methods on engineering drawings and provides a feasible digital 
modeling solution for the old towers that lack complete drawings; second, it shortens 
the modeling cycle time for a single tower, which significantly improves the effi-
ciency of large-scale inspections of the power grid. This “computer vision + finite 
element” fusion paradigm provides a new technical path for the rapid safety assess-
ment of electric power infrastructure, which is especially suitable for engineering sce-
narios such as post-disaster emergency assessment and tower monitoring in remote 
areas. 
(4) Limitations and Future Work: Current limitations include lighting-dependent recon-
struction accuracy and predefined material assumptions. The current method relies 
on a predefined database for cross-sectional properties and does not automatically 
identify member geometries from point clouds. Structural defect recognition (e.g., 
bolt loosening, member deformation) is also not addressed in this study. Future work 
will explore automated cross-section recognition through point cloud geometric 
analysis or deep learning-based classification, as well as the integration of defect de-
tection techniques into the point-cloud-to-FEM pipeline. Future work will also inte-
grate LiDAR–thermal fusion and deep learning for robust automated modeling, par-
ticularly for aged infrastructure and tower-line systems. 
Overall, this approach provides insights and methodologies for the application of 
computer vision to structural FEA and serves as a reference for developing software re-
lated to FEM using computer vision technology. Improving modeling accuracy and auto-
mation is a focus and direction for future work in this research.
