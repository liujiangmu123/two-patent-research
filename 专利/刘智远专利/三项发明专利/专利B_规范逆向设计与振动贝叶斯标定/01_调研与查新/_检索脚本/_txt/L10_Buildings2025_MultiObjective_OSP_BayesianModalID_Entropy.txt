Abstract: Structural Health Monitoring relies on accurate modal identification and effective
damage detection to assess structural performance and safety. However, traditional sensor
placement methods struggle to balance modal identification uncertainty, which arises from
limited sensor coverage and measurement noise and damage detection sensitivity, which
requires sensors to be optimally positioned to capture structural stiffness variations. To
address this challenge, this study proposes a multi-objective sensor placement optimization
method based on the Non-Dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm. The method introduces
two key objective functions: minimizing modal identification uncertainty by leveraging
Bayesian modal identification theory and information entropy and maximizing damage de-
tection sensitivity by incorporating an entropy-based measure to quantify the uncertainty in
stiffness variation estimation. By formulating the problem as Pareto-based multi-objective
optimization, the method efficiently explores a trade-off between the two competing ob-
jectives and provides a diverse set of optimal sensor placement solutions. The proposed
approach is validated through numerical experiments on a simply supported beam and
a benchmark bridge structure, demonstrating that different optimization objectives lead
to distinct sensor placement patterns. The results show that solutions prioritizing modal
identification distribute sensors across the structure to improve global response estimation,
while solutions favoring damage detection concentrate sensors in critical areas to enhance
sensitivity. The proposed method significantly improves sensor placement strategies by
offering a systematic and flexible framework for SHM applications, enabling engineers to
tailor monitoring strategies based on specific structural assessment needs.
Keywords: multi-objective sensor placement; modal identification uncertainty; damage
detection sensitivity; Pareto optimization; non-dominated sorting genetic algorithm
1. Introduction
The safety and functionality of civil infrastructure are continuously assessed through
Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) systems, which collect and analyze real-time struc-
tural response data to provide critical insights [1,2]. The accurate identification of modal
parameters, such as natural frequencies, mode shapes, and modal coordinates, is a cru-
cial component of SHM. These parameters play a key role in system identification, finite
element model updating, and structural damage detection [3–5]. Structural damage is
typically manifested as variations in these modal parameters, indicating potential changes
in stiffness, mass, or damping characteristics [6]. However, the precision of modal pa-
rameter identification and damage detection is inherently constrained by the quality and

spatial distribution of sensor data, which can be affected by sensor placement limitations
and measurement noise [7–9]. In practical engineering applications, such as bridges and
high-rise buildings, researchers often need to develop optimized placement strategies
for different types of sensors, including displacement and acceleration sensors, based on
practical requirements and specific structural characteristics, in order to improve structural
data collection [10–12]. Even when the number of sensors is limited, these strategies can
still ensure accurate and consistent modal parameter identification.
The optimization of sensor placement for modal parameters has been widely explored
in an effort to improve modal identification accuracy. This research primarily aims to mini-
mize uncertainties stemming from modeling errors and measurement noise. Among these
approaches, the Effective Independence (EI) method has emerged as a widely adopted strat-
egy, selecting sensor locations by minimizing the Fisher information matrix’s determinant,
which is directly related to the covariance matrix of modal coordinate estimators [13]. This
method has been further extended to support multidimensional sensor layouts, where mul-
tiple rows in the mode shape matrix are linked to a single sensor location [14]. Additionally,
the relationship between the EI method and the kinetic energy (KE) method, which incorpo-
rates the mass matrix into the Fisher information matrix to enhance sensor selection criteria,
has been explored [15]. Recent advancements have introduced Bayesian statistical frame-
works into sensor placement optimization, leveraging probabilistic uncertainty modeling to
improve robustness. In particular, information entropy has been proposed as a scalar metric
for quantifying uncertainty, providing a rigorous theoretical basis for optimizing sensor
configurations [16,17]. Furthermore, novel strategies integrating heterogeneous sensor
types have been developed to enhance measurement accuracy. Pei et al. [18] proposed a
hybrid placement method that optimizes the deployment of strain and acceleration sensors
to minimize the uncertainty in displacement mode shape estimation. Similarly, Zhang
and Xu [19] incorporated discrete Kalman filtering into sensor placement optimization,
facilitating sensor arrangement strategies tailored for both response reconstruction and
excitation reconstruction scenarios.
Damage detection-oriented sensor placement methods aim to strategically position
sensors to enhance the identification of structural stiffness changes while improving modal
identification accuracy. Among these approaches, frequency-based damage identification
methods have been widely utilized due to their computational efficiency and simplicity.
Early studies primarily relied on natural frequency shifts as indicators of structural dam-
age [20,21]. However, compared to frequency-based approaches, mode shape analysis
offers greater sensitivity to localized stiffness variations due to its spatially distributed
characteristics [22,23].
To further refine damage detection, Yang [24] introduced information entropy as a
measure to assess uncertainties arising from stiffness variations, treating them as model
errors. Hiramoto et al. [25] applied the explicit solution of the algebraic Riccati equation to
optimize both sensor and actuator placements for active vibration control, demonstrating
an integrated approach to monitoring and control. Worden and Burrows [26] leveraged
curvature data to develop novel sensor distribution methods, emphasizing the role of
spatial derivatives in detecting structural anomalies. Additionally, Shi et al. [27] and Guo
et al. [28] proposed sensor layouts specifically designed to detect and localize structural
damage using test modal data, an area that remains relatively underexplored in sensor
placement research. Further developments in damage-oriented sensor placement include
the work of Pei et al. [29], who treated potential structural damage as modeling errors,
adjusting sensor configurations to enhance modal identification. However, while their
study optimized sensor placement for modal identification accuracy, it did not explicitly
evaluate the impact of sensor placement on damage detection sensitivity or establish a

comprehensive framework that simultaneously addresses modal identification uncertainty
and damage detection sensitivity. This gap highlights the need for a more integrated
optimization strategy that considers both objectives within a unified framework.
Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) necessitates multi-objective optimization meth-
ods to balance competing demands, a challenge that has gained increasing attention among
researchers worldwide. To address this, various studies have explored advanced optimiza-
tion strategies for sensor placement. Sankary and Ostfeld [30] introduced a multi-objective
approach for wireless sensor placement, enhancing the quality of modal information while
minimizing energy consumption. Soman et al. [31] proposed an optimization framework
that considers both structural modal identification and mode shape expansion, demon-
strating its applicability to long-span bridges. Similarly, Cha et al. [32] utilized a genetic
algorithm to refine the positioning of active control devices and sensors within a structural
framework, achieving both cost reductions and improved control performance. Further
advancements in Pareto-based sensor placement optimization have been proposed to ad-
dress the complexity of multi-objective trade-offs. Nong et al. [33] developed a method
incorporating Pareto optimization to simultaneously satisfy multiple modal parameter
estimation criteria. This approach employed the Non-Dominated Sorting Genetic Algo-
rithm II (NSGA-II) to efficiently compute the Pareto front, enabling the selection of sensor
configurations that optimally balance competing objectives [34]. However, as the number
of sensor placement evaluation criteria increases, the computational burden associated
with exhaustive search methods becomes prohibitively large, rendering them impractical
for large-scale structures. Consequently, intelligent algorithms have been widely adopted
to identify near-optimal sensor configurations while maintaining an acceptable level of
accuracy [35–37].
In this study, sensor placement criteria are separately formulated to address modal
identification uncertainty and damage detection sensitivity, and a Pareto-based multi-
objective optimization algorithm using the Non-Dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm
(NSGA-II) is proposed. First, Bayesian modal identification theory is applied to establish
a sensor placement criterion that minimizes uncertainty in modal coordinate estimation,
enhancing the accuracy and reliability of modal parameter identification. Next, leveraging
the relationship between modal parameter variations and stiffness damage coefficients, a
second sensor placement criterion is introduced to improve damage detection sensitivity
by minimizing uncertainty in the identification of damage coefficients. To effectively
balance these two competing objectives, NSGA-II is employed to construct a multi-objective
optimization framework that yields a collection of Pareto-optimal sensor configurations,
enabling a trade-off between modal identification accuracy and damage detectability.
Finally, the proposed method is validated through numerical studies on a simply supported
beam and a benchmark bridge structure, demonstrating its effectiveness in generating
sensor configurations tailored to different monitoring priorities. Additionally, the interplay
between modal identification uncertainty and damage detection sensitivity under identical
sensor placement scenarios is analyzed to provide deeper insight into their mutual influence
and trade-offs.

2.3. Multi-Objective Optimization Method
In sensor placement optimization, when both modal identification uncertainty and
damage detection sensitivity must be considered simultaneously, single-objective opti-
mization methods often fail to effectively balance these competing factors. To address this
challenge, Pareto optimization is introduced to establish a multi-objective sensor placement
criterion, allowing for a comprehensive evaluation of how these two objectives influence
the optimal sensor configuration. The core concept of Pareto optimization is to solve for the
Pareto front, which yields a collection of non-dominated solutions, capturing the trade-offs
between the two objectives rather than converging to a single globally optimal outcome.
The Pareto front ensures that no other solution can simultaneously outperform a given so-
lution in both objectives, thereby providing multiple optimized sensor placement strategies
for decision-making. However, solving for the Pareto front poses significant computational
challenges. Traditional global search algorithms often involve excessive computational
costs, while sequential optimization methods tend to become trapped in local optima. To
improve computational efficiency and obtain high-quality sensor placement solutions, this
study employs the NSGA-II. The proposed approach consists of four key steps:
(1)
Discrete Encoding of Candidate Sensor Locations: A set of finite element model
nodes are selected as candidate measurement positions, ensuring that potential sensor
locations are well distributed across the structure. To represent sensor placement
decisions, a binary encoding scheme (0, 1) is adopted, where ‘1’ indicates that a sensor
is placed at a given node and ‘0’ means no sensor is placed at that node. This encoding
forms the sensor selection matrix L, which is shown in Equation (1).
(2)
Initial Population Generation and Evolution: An initial population of sensor place-
ment configurations is randomly generated, and their bi-objective function values are
computed. Through selection, crossover, and mutation operations, new solutions are
iteratively generated, allowing the population to evolve over successive generations.
(3)
Non-Dominated Sorting and Crowding Distance Computation: the parent and off-
spring populations are merged, and non-dominated sorting, along with crowding
distance measures, is applied to select superior individuals, maintaining both the
quality and diversity of Pareto front solutions.
(4)
Convergence Assessment and Termination: The process is repeated until a termination
threshold is reached, either upon hitting the maximum generation count or when

Buildings 2025, 15, 821
7 of 15
population convergence is achieved. If the termination criterion is not satisfied, the
optimization process continues until a stable Pareto front solution set is obtained.
By leveraging this multi-objective optimization framework, a reasonable balance be-
tween modal identification uncertainty and damage detection sensitivity can be achieved.
The proposed approach provides an optimized sensor placement strategy for SHM applica-
tions, facilitating more efficient and reliable monitoring of engineering structures.
3. Case Study Analysis
3.1. The Simply Supported Beam
The proposed sensor placement method is first applied to a simply supported beam
numerical model to analyze the characteristics of sensor distributions corresponding to
modal identification uncertainty and damage detection sensitivity. Additionally, the mutual
influence between these two objectives on sensor placement is investigated. The simply
supported beam model has a Young’s modulus of 212 GPa, a density of 7456 kg/m3, and
cross-sectional dimensions of 50 mm × 15.62 mm [29]. The beam has a total length of 1.9 m
and is discretized into 19 finite elements using planar beam elements (Ne = 19). As shown
in Figure 1, the 18 vertical degrees of freedom (DOFs) at the nodes serve as candidate sensor
locations. The first three modal shapes are selected as the target modes for identification.
Since Rayleigh damping preserves the mode shapes for both damped and undamped cases,
the damping effect does not influence the mode shape selection. To ensure accurate modal
identification, the number of sensors must exceed the number of target modes. Thus, the
number of deployed sensors is set to five to ensure adequate monitoring coverage, with the
objective functions defined as follows: Objective Function 1 focuses on minimizing modal
identification uncertainty, as formulated in Equation (6), while Objective Function 2 aims
to maximize damage detection sensitivity, as formulated in Equation (14).

axis represents Objective Function 1 (modal identification uncertainty) and the vertical axis
represents Objective Function 2 (damage detection sensitivity). In the figure, red-filled circles
denote Pareto front solutions, while blue-filled circles indicate locally optimal solutions
obtained during the optimization process. By analyzing the Pareto front and locally optimal

3.3. Discussion
In the finite element model of a structure, changes in the stiffness matrix are typically
used to represent structural variations. Generally, the overall stiffness matrix change is
obtained by accumulating the changes in individual element stiffness matrices, which
are typically caused by material degradation or constraint variations, both of which are
reflected in the variation coefficients of the element stiffness matrices. In our study, we
simulate structural damage by reducing the stiffness of selected finite element components
in both the simply supported beam model and the benchmark bridge model according to
damage coefficients. In damage detection research, it is commonly assumed that the damage
coefficient lies between 0.1 and 0.3. Notably, when the covariance matrix is assumed to be a
diagonal matrix with equal diagonal elements, the absolute values of the assumed damage
coefficients do not affect the relative ranking of different sensor placements. Furthermore,
Equation (14) is a simplified form, assuming that the variations in different stiffness matrix
coefficients are similar. If there are significant differences in the variation patterns, Equation
(13) can be used to derive the partial derivative of the damage coefficient, which can then
be substituted into Equation (4) for further analysis.
Previous studies on structural parameter identification (including modal and physical
parameters) have examined the impact of damage on parameter estimation and further
evaluated its influence on sensor placement strategies. The findings indicate that when
damage detection sensitivity is not considered and only parameter identification uncer-
tainty is considered in a single-objective framework, the presence of structural damage
has little impact on sensor placement [29]. On the one hand, in computational analysis,
the effect of damage is often modeled as variations in noise variance or as a random
distribution. These assumptions do not introduce significant fluctuations into the data
distribution and, consequently, do not lead to substantial differences in the computational
results [13,17,24,27,28]. On the other hand, as observed in this study, even when damage
effects are considered, the optimization criteria remain focused on structural parameter
estimation, fundamentally maintaining a single-objective optimization framework. Since
the objective function remains unchanged and the structural information contained in
the selected sensor locations varies only slightly, the sensor placement strategy remains
largely unaffected. However, the multi-objective optimization approach proposed in this
study reveals that the selection of sensor locations is influenced by the interplay between
the two objectives. Given that different objective functions exhibit distinct computational
tendencies, the information captured at different sensor locations also varies, leading to
an interdependent selection process. It is important to note that this interaction does not
always manifest as a conflicting relationship, as it depends on the definition of the objective
functions and the correlation between the data used in the computation.
4. Conclusions
This study proposes a multi-objective sensor placement method based on the NSGA-II
to balance modal identification uncertainty and damage detection sensitivity in SHM. By
introducing information entropy to quantify the uncertainty in both modal coordinate
estimation and stiffness variation assessment, the method provides a systematic framework
for optimizing sensor layouts. Numerical validation on a simply supported beam and a
benchmark bridge structure confirms its effectiveness in generating sensor configurations
tailored to different monitoring priorities.
A key innovation of this study is the use of information entropy to evaluate the
uncertainty in stiffness variation estimation, which enhances the assessment of damage
detection sensitivity. By integrating this approach within the optimization framework, the
method enables a more robust and adaptive selection of sensor placements, improving both

14 of 15
the accuracy and reliability of structural damage identification. The results show that sensor
layouts optimized for damage detection tend to cluster in regions where stiffness changes
are most pronounced, effectively enhancing sensitivity to localized structural deterioration.
Conversely, configurations prioritizing modal identification are more evenly distributed,
ensuring comprehensive modal response capture but potentially reducing sensitivity to
local damage. This interplay highlights the inherent trade-off between the two objectives,
where optimizing one may come at the expense of the other.
Additionally, the Pareto-based optimization framework provides a range of trade-
off solutions, allowing engineers to select sensor placements that best align with specific
monitoring requirements. The results indicate that while balanced sensor distributions
ensure adequate performance for both objectives, targeted configurations can be fine-tuned
to either enhance modal identification accuracy or increase sensitivity to structural damage.
This adaptability ensures that the method can be effectively applied to various SHM
scenarios with different constraints and priorities.
Due to time and capability constraints, there are still areas in this study that can be
further explored. Future research could incorporate physical parameter identification,
time-series prediction, and correlation analysis to formulate additional objective functions.
By utilizing Pareto optimization methods, the interactions between different monitoring
objectives in sensor placement can be analyzed, leading to further optimization of sensor
placement strategies.
