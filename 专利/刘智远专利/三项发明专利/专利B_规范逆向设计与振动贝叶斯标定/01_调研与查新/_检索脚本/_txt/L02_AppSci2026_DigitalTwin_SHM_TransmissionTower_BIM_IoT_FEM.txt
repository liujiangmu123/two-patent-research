Abstract
Transmission towers, as critical infrastructure in power systems, are frequently threatened
by multiple hazards such as strong winds and flood scour. Traditional structural health
monitoring methods face limitations in data feedback timeliness and mechanical interpre-
tation, making real-time condition awareness and early warning under disaster scenarios
challenging. To address these issues, this paper proposes a digital twin framework for
transmission tower structures, integrating Building Information Modeling (BIM), Internet
of Things (IoT) technology, and the Finite Element Method (FEM) for structural health
monitoring and visual warning under wind loads and flood scour effects. The framework
achieves cross-platform collaboration through the FEM Open Application Programming
Interface (OAPI) and Python scripts. In the physical domain, fluctuating wind loads are
simulated based on the Davenport spectrum, flood scour depth is modeled using the HEC-
18 formulation, and foundation constraint degradation is represented through nonlinear
spring stiffness reduction. In the FEM domain, dynamic time-history analyses are con-
ducted to obtain structural responses. In the BIM domain, a three-level warning mechanism
based on stress change rate (∆R) is established to achieve intuitive rendering and dynamic
feedback of structural damage. A 44.4 m high latticed angle steel tower is employed as
the case study for validation. Results demonstrate that the simulated wind spectrum
closely matches the theoretical target spectrum, confirming the validity of the load input. A
critical scour evolution threshold of 40% is identified, beyond which the first two natural
frequencies exhibit nonlinear decay with a maximum reduction of 80.9%. Non-uniform
scour induces significant load transfer, with axial forces at leeside nodes increasing from
27 kN to 54 kN. During the 0–60 s wind loading process, BIM visualization accurately
captures the full stress evolution from the tower base to the upper structure, showing
excellent agreement with FEM results. The proposed framework establishes a closed-loop
interaction mechanism of “physical sensing–digital simulation–visual warning”, effectively
enhancing the timeliness and interpretability of structural health monitoring for trans-
mission towers under multiple hazards, providing an innovative approach for intelligent
disaster prevention in power infrastructure.

1. Introduction
Transmission towers serve as lifelines ensuring the normal operation of national
power systems, and their safety and stability directly affect urban functioning. Angle
steel towers, as a common type of transmission tower, are constructed from angle steel
sections, with main members, bracing members, and auxiliary members forming a spatial
truss structure connected by bolts. Due to their ability to fully utilize material compressive
performance and their relatively small slenderness ratios, such latticed structures are widely
used in urban and mountainous transmission lines, particularly suitable for voltage levels
of 110–500 kV. Angle steel towers often exceed 30 m in height, classifying them as typical
high-rise structures [1]. In recent years, wind and flood disasters have frequently occurred
in urban and mountainous areas. Wind load, as the primary controlling load, often leads to
the collapse or severe deformation of angle steel towers [2–7]; meanwhile, tower foundation
failures caused by flood scour are also common, as documented in references [8–10]. Once
a transmission tower is damaged, power grid system failures can easily trigger blackouts,
not only seriously threatening people’s lives and property but also hindering national
industrial development. Thus, transmission towers face the dual threat of strong winds
and floods.
With the increasing frequency of disasters, higher requirements have been placed on
the wind and flood resistance of transmission tower structures, making structural health
monitoring an increasingly effective approach. Due to the high cost, destructive nature,
and irreversibility of physical experiments, structures can typically only be equipped
with physical sensors to measure mechanical response parameters such as strain and
displacement. However, in actual post-disaster scenarios, only post-event analysis is
usually possible, while pre-disaster prevention and real-time monitoring have long been
difficult to achieve. To optimize monitoring schemes before actual deployment, Internet
of Things (IoT) technology has gained widespread attention and application in scientific
research [11–15]. By deploying virtual sensors in structures, the functions of physical
sensors can be simulated, outputting mechanical parameters at terminals. Compared to
physical sensors, virtual sensors have extremely low costs, enabling large-scale virtual
deployment and data feedback, offering significant advantages for pre-disaster monitoring.
Additionally, Building Information Modeling (BIM) technology is also regarded as a reliable
tool for structural health monitoring.
In the interaction process between the IoT and BIM, traditional workflows typically
use BIM as a visualization interface and sensor feedback platform, while the IoT converts
physical parameters into output parameters, packages them for upload to cloud servers,
and then relies on manual judgment to determine whether structural damage has occurred.
Although this approach has improved work efficiency to some extent, several non-negligible
issues remain. First, traditional IoT systems typically serve only as passive data acquisition
and transmission terminals, lacking the ability to autonomously interpret the mechanical
mechanisms behind abnormal data [16–19]. This disconnects the monitoring data from
the physical relationships with specific disaster scenarios (such as flood scour and strong
winds), making fault diagnosis heavily dependent on manual post-event analysis and
unable to achieve real-time traceability of disaster causes. Second, current monitoring
systems are isolated from traditional structural mechanics analysis programs, making it
difficult to achieve dynamic interaction with professional structural analysis software such
as SAP2000, Abaqus, and Ansys, thus preventing real-time simulation.
Therefore, existing structural health monitoring technologies urgently need upgrading.
By achieving dynamic interaction between FEM and IoT/BIM, the limitations of tradi-
tional monitoring approaches—namely, the disconnection between data and mechanical
analysis—can be effectively overcome. The deep integration of IoT monitoring technology

with structural simulation, by constructing a digital twin system with dynamic perception

To fulfill the above objectives, this paper proposes a digital twin framework for
transmission tower systems, aiming to enable real-time monitoring and visual warning of
structural health under wind and flood disasters. The framework seamlessly integrates
Building Information Modeling (BIM), Internet of Things (IoT) technology, and the Finite
Element Method (FEM) through the Open Application Programming Interface (OAPI) and
Python (version 3.13) scripts. Operating as a near real-time, simulation-driven system, the
framework accounts for computational latency; in this workflow, virtual sensor outputs
automatically trigger FEM re-analysis. The total latency is approximately 3–5 min per
cycle, with synchronization maintained through a timestamp-matching script to ensure
data consistency between hazard inputs and structural responses. In the physical domain,
fluctuating wind loads described by the Davenport spectrum and flood scour effects
simulated using the HEC-18 model are represented. In the FEM domain, dynamic time-
history analyses are conducted to obtain structural responses. In the BIM domain, a
three-level warning mechanism based on stress change rates is established to provide
intuitive rendering and dynamic feedback of structural damage. Through validation on
a 44.4-m-high angle steel tower, the framework effectively simulates structural behavior
under wind and flood actions and achieves closed-loop interaction from “physical sensing”
to “digital response”, providing a novel technical pathway for early damage identification
and health monitoring of transmission towers in multi-hazard environments.
Compared to traditional FEM–IoT approaches that primarily output isolated numerical
data, the proposed framework seamlessly maps real-time mechanical responses onto 3D
BIM. This integration transforms abstract structural analyses into a spatially aware digital
twin, enabling intuitive and multi-dimensional safety assessments under complex hazard
scenarios [20,21].

framework, mechanical models accurately simulating wind loads and flood scour effects
are established through FEM programs, employing wind load models, flood scour models,
and soil–structure interaction (SSI) models recommended by international standards.

3. Digital Twin System Framework
3.1. Overview of Digital Twin Architecture
To clearly present the workflow of the digital twin framework in this paper, its working
flowchart is shown in Figure 1. The main structure of the digital twin framework consists
of three parts: Physical Entity Domain (Part 1), FEM Simulation Domain (Part 2), and
Data and Control Center (Part 3). These three parts work together through the FEM
Open Application Programming Interface (OAPI) and Python code programs. Each part
works independently while being interconnected. Specifically, the FEM program from
CSI (USA) is used to establish the finite element model of the transmission tower and
complete structural calculations. Subsequently, the validated model data are imported
into Revit 2025 BIM software to automatically generate a three-dimensional solid model,
thereby using the BIM model as the visual window for terminal monitoring. The OAPI
program acts as a “connector” in this process, ensuring efficient collaborative work among
different application programs. Additionally, FEM calculation results can be mapped to
the BIM program through the OAPI to establish a visual warning mechanism, achieving
real-time monitoring effects. The system can automatically identify and capture abnormal
data based on set thresholds and highlight risky structural components through rendering,
achieving visual warning effects. The Python program acts as a binding agent in this
process, enabling API call transmission work. It can achieve automated workflows through
code language, including opening the FEM, setting analysis cases, extracting calculation
results, and outputting them to data processing programs such as Excel, reducing the
cumbersome processes of traditional finite element operations, improving data operation
and processing efficiency, and achieving real-time interaction between the physical tower
and the finite element model.

3.2.2. Implementation of Flood Scour in OAPI and Python
To simulate the effects of mountainous flood scour on transmission tower foundations
in the OAPI program, we adopt the simplified treatment of pile–soil interaction derived
in Section 2 through Equations (5) and (6) in the Python program. Specifically, equivalent
spring elements are used for the simulation at foundation nodes. Unlike the wind load
application process, the flood scour model is mainly implemented by setting boundary
conditions through the Set Multi Linear Points module in the OAPI program. Equation (5)
can be used in the program to calculate scour depth, while Equation (6) calculates and
updates the soil’s support capacity for the tower foundation. By updating the spring link
properties in the FEM OAPI program, an effect similar to a stiffness reduction caused by

3.3. IoT-OAPI-Based Finite Element Simulation Domain (Part 2)
In this study, the transmission tower is modeled using finite element (FE) software
(SAP2000 V24). Virtual sensors are implemented by targeting specific node IDs to extract
structural responses, such as nodal forces and accelerations, via a Python-based Open
Application Programming Interface (OAPI). This approach effectively simulates the data
output of physical sensors. Consequently, it establishes a direct linkage between the
dynamic monitoring data and simulated disaster scenarios (e.g., flood-induced scour and
strong winds), enabling an interactive evaluation of the tower’s mechanical behavior under
dynamic monitoring conditions.
In the visual warning interface, as shown in Figure 4, when simulating flood scour
conditions, once the scour depth exceeds the set critical threshold, the boundary stiffness
immediately degrades, thereby triggering internal force redistribution in the transmission
tower foundation to simulate scour effects. The Python program automatically monitors
the above mechanical responses through the OAPI interface. When monitoring those
components, if stress ratios exceed safety limits, the system immediately identifies them as
risky components, adds a red rendering display, and sends trigger instructions to the BIM.
The corresponding digital elements in the BIM environment then automatically update to
rendered bright-colored materials, providing intuitive structural health warnings. When
simulating extreme wind load conditions, the transmission tower structure in FEM needs
to be monitored according to control stress ratios and deflection limits. Through load data
transmitted from the physical entity domain output and calculated by the FEM program,
overall structural stress nephograms can be generated. When stress ratios are less than set
limits or nodal displacements exceed code-specified limits, the same mapping mechanism
as in flood conditions is triggered—corresponding components update to rendered material
colors, providing warnings at the BIM visualization terminal. When wind loads and flood
scour act simultaneously, the system makes judgments according to the envelope design,
issuing warnings as soon as design requirements are not met.

3.4. Data Control Center Domain (Part 3)
Serving as the core “connector” and “transporter” within the digital twin architecture,
the Data and Control Center (Part 3) seamlessly bridges virtual sensor data streams with
finite element (FE) simulation results. By leveraging a Python-based framework alongside
the lightweight MQTT protocol, the center establishes a stable, asynchronous transmission
topology tailored for real-time data delivery. Furthermore, the integration of advanced
control logic and verification algorithms enables real-time compliance screening of incom-
ing data via the FEM–Python–BIM visualization interface. Ultimately, this automated
closed-loop system efficiently manages complex data routing while significantly reducing
manual labor costs.
4. Finite Element Analysis
Addressing the pain point of feedback lag in traditional structural monitoring for
disaster prevention and control, this paper proposes a digital twin interactive monitor-
ing framework for transmission towers, aiming to achieve real-time synchronization of
structural mechanical behavior and dynamic response. To validate this method, this study
selected a widely used angle steel tower in power grids as a typical analysis object. Such
high-rise structures are extremely sensitive to wind-induced vibrations, and the mechanical
boundaries of their discrete members are very clear, highly compatible with the design
method proposed in this paper. Through this model, research work was conducted, deeply
integrating IoT sensing technology with three-dimensional information models and the fi-
nite element analysis software FEM. With the help of customized application programming
interfaces, the cross-domain barrier from “physical-end IoT real-time data acquisition” to
“virtual-end structural force deduction” was broken through, providing a new approach
for early damage monitoring of transmission tower systems.
This study selected a lattice transmission tower as the research case, as shown in
Figure 5. Specifically, the structure was divided into three main parts: the tower head, the
tower body, and the foundation. In the head and body, all structural elements were made of
L-shaped equal-leg angle steel. This included the main, diagonal, and auxiliary members.
Furthermore, these steel members featured 20 different cross-sectional sizes, ranging from
L40 × 3 to L140 × 10.
This study focused on a 44.4 m high latticed angle steel transmission tower. Specifically,
the basic modeling parameters of this structure are detailed in Table 2. The primary material
used was standard structural steel. In addition, the input material density was amplified
to an equivalent mass density. This adjustment accounted for the weight of auxiliary
components, such as gusset plates, bolted connections, climbing ladders, and anti-corrosion
galvanized layers.

4.1. Simulation of Random Wind-Induced Vibration Effects on High-Rise Transmission Towers

4.1.1. Fluctuating Wind Speed Simulation and Power Spectral Density Verification
This paper used the Davenport function from Section 3.2.1 as the theoretical basis for
the frequency domain characteristics of wind loads. To meet the input requirements for time-
history analysis, this paper simulated the time history of fluctuating wind speed at target
nodes based on the harmonic superposition method. Input dynamic wind load parameters
are shown in Table 3. The extracted wind speed time-history data underwent a fast Fourier
transform using the Welch method [33,34] to obtain the auto-power spectrum (simulation)
function curve, which was compared and verified with the Davenport theoretical target
spectrum (theoretical), as shown in Figure 6. Results indicated that the simulated power
spectrum curve closely matched the target power spectrum curve, proving that the power
spectrum time-history function generated in this paper satisfied the random characteristics
of natural wind in terms of energy distribution, providing a reliable load input boundary
for subsequent OAPI structural time-history calculations.
As shown in Figure 7, the simulated wind speed fluctuates dramatically around the
average wind speed of 36.1 m/s (red dashed line in the figure) at the height of the target
node (Node 361), reflecting the randomness and fluctuating characteristics of natural wind.
Figure 8 shows the dynamic wind load time-history curve generated by this fluctuating
wind speed acting on the structural target node 361. The simulated spectrum closely
matches the target Davenport spectrum in the main energy frequency band (0.1 Hz to
10 Hz), reflecting the high correlation between the fluctuation trend of dynamic wind force
and the time-history characteristics of wind speed. To rigorously quantify the agreement

4.1.2. Structural Dynamic Response Time-History Analysis
To verify the data interaction process of the digital twin framework, this paper used
the wind force time-history curve generated in Section 4.1.1 as environmental excitation.
These load data were published in real time as a continuous data stream relying on the
MQTT protocol. Simultaneously, the FEM automatically read the above time-series data
through the OAPI interface, thereby dynamically driving the structure to conduct a wind-
induced vibration time-history analysis and subsequent flood scour analysis. Furthermore,
for accurate comparison, “virtual sensors” were set at typical nodes at the tower base.
These nodes strictly corresponded spatially to the IoT monitoring sensors of the on-site
physical structure, and their main function was to synchronously extract and calculate
the dynamic displacement responses and stress data of the tower in real time within the
numerical model.
The wind-induced time-history curves generated in Section 4.1.1 were applied to the
FEM model to conduct the dynamic time-history response analysis. To ensure the real-time
computational efficiency of the proposed digital twin framework within its closed-loop in-
teraction, geometric nonlinearities (P-∆and large displacement effects) were not considered
in this analysis. Existing studies [37] have also demonstrated that the influence of geometric
nonlinearity on the dynamic characteristics of transmission towers is negligible in such
cases. Consequently, obtaining the dynamic response solution without superimposing
geometric nonlinearity effectively satisfied the low-latency requirements of the digital twin
system while maintaining the requisite accuracy for stress and displacement calculations.
The wind force time-history curve from Section 4.1.1 was applied to the FEM model.
This step facilitated a dynamic time-history analysis of the wind load. Specifically, it
investigated the tower’s displacement patterns under wind-induced vibration. For this
analysis, three representative nodes were selected: the tower base (Node 80), the body
(Node 400), and the top (Node 361). Subsequently, their displacement responses were
extracted and plotted as time-history curves, as illustrated in Figure 9.

4.2. Foundation Stiffness Attenuation and Nonlinear Evolution of Boundary Conditions Induced
by Flood Scour
When conducting numerical modeling in the Python environment, the determination
of foundation stiffness must consider the effects of scour on the structure. The classical
API specification p-y curve model (as shown in Equation (6)) reveals that the lateral soil
resistance of the foundation is positively correlated with soil depth z. When flood scour
occurs, the surface soil is directly stripped away under flood loads, which not only causes
a loss of rigid constraint at the tower foundation but also leads to a sharp decrease in the
effective confining pressure of the underlying soil at the foundation burial depth. Due to
the reduction in calculated depth z, the soil undergoes accelerated softening, exhibiting
significant nonlinear attenuation characteristics.
Reference [39] indicates that when scour exceeds a critical depth, it triggers abrupt
changes in soil–structure interaction boundaries, leading to nonlinear accelerated decline
in structural natural frequencies. Reference [40] shows that severe scour can lead to almost
complete loss of lateral foundation constraints, subsequently causing rigid body rotation.
The above literature collectively demonstrates that traditional linear stiffness reduction
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16083620

Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 3620

methods cannot truly simulate the cliff-like abrupt changes in boundary conditions during
the later stages of scour.
Given the high sensitivity of high-rise transmission towers to overturning stiffness,
this study nonlinearly mapped the degree of scour to an exponential reduction in equivalent
spring stiffness, as shown in Equation (13), thereby realistically reproducing the evolution
of the foundation from “elastic fixity” to a “near-hinged state” in the finite element environ-
ment. Through this approach, this study simulated the entire process of the transmission
tower from local instability to eventual collapse, aiming to reveal the nonlinear dynamic
evolution laws of high-rise structures under extreme scour conditions. The resulting flood
scour parameter table is shown in Table 4.

The nonlinear mapping in Equation (13) is grounded in the stress-dependency theory
of soil stiffness. According to [41], the removal of overburden soil layers during scour leads
to a catastrophic reduction in effective confining pressure, causing a nonlinear degradation
of the soil’s reaction modulus.
In the FEM model, the initial reference stiffness K0 was set to 107 kN/m. This value
was determined based on the standard parameters for elastic fixity provided in [42]. It
ensured sufficient overturning resistance in the un-scoured state. The dimensionless scalar
Sc (ranging from zero to one) defines the evolution from the pristine to the ultimate scour
state. The decay coefficient α captures the geotechnical sensitivity of the site, simulating
the transition from a rigid constraint to a near-hinged condition. To align with the API
p-y framework, foundation degradation was modeled by attenuating both translational
(U1, U2, U3) and rotational (R1, R2, R3) stiffness. Using nodal springs in FEM instead
of solid elements ensured soil–structure interaction (SSI) fidelity while maintaining high
computational efficiency.
In natural rivers or floods, scour never occurs uniformly; the scour experienced by
foundations often exhibits certain randomness and non-uniformity. To determine the
influence patterns of different scour distribution modes on transmission tower stability in
the simulation, we designed two comparative working conditions—uniform scour and non-
uniform scour—based on the above exponential degradation model. Under uniform scour

the spatial stiffness distribution characteristics. Uniform scour and non-uniform scour

Furthermore, changes in base bending moments also reflect that the structure is in a
dangerous state under scour. For upstream nodes 45 and 47, as the soil at the upstream
foundation is gradually scoured, they cannot generate reaction forces to resist rotation, so
the bending moment values are rapidly released and tend toward zero. This indicates that
the upstream supports have lost their constraint capacity on the tower legs, changing from
fixed to hinged. For downstream nodes 49 and 51, under non-uniform scour conditions,
they need to anchor the tower body attempting to tilt. This strong constraint causes the
bending moment values at nodes 49 and 51 to soar to 183 kN·m. This bending moment
indicates that downstream nodes must not only resist compression but also bending, which
can easily lead to “stiffness eccentricity”, causing the shearing of tower footing connection
bolts or buckling of main members.
Further observation of Figure 13 reveals that when the equivalent scour severity (i.e.,
stiffness degradation) exceeds 40%, the natural frequency curves of the structure exhibit a
distinct inflection point, followed by a sharp nonlinear decline. This evolutionary feature
is highly consistent with established geotechnical dynamics. Reference [29] demonstrates
that the natural frequency of a pile affected by scour does not decay linearly with scour
depth; instead, it undergoes a sharp, nonlinear drop only after the scour reaches a critical
threshold. Based on that reference and the curve mutation observed in Figure 13, this study
identified 40% as the critical evolutionary threshold for the dynamic degradation of the
transmission tower foundation under scour.
Figure 15 shows that non-uniform scour induces significant internal force transforma-
tion effects. On the one hand, at the axial force level, upstream and downstream nodes
exhibit highly symmetrical force transfer. As scour increases from 20% to 80%, the load
contribution rates show a “scissors difference” evolution (from 25% shifting to 24.3% and
25.7% respectively), reflecting the dangerous migration of several tons of gravity load to
the safe side. On the other hand, at the bending moment level, redistribution is even more
pronounced: the contribution rate of upstream foundations plummets below 10% in the
early stage of scour (20%) due to the loss of rotational constraint, almost losing their role as
fixed supports. When scour reaches 80% depth, the two downstream supports together
bear nearly 100% of the bending moment load of the entire tower. Under this condition,
the downstream foundation is more prone to failure and overall collapse. Due to the loss of
stiffness at upstream supports (nodes 45, 47) causing an internal force release, the load is

4.3. FEM–BIM Result Mapping and Dynamic Visual Warning of Structural Risks Based on OAPI
This study constructed an automated analysis pipeline across FEM–BIM platforms.
First, FEM raw data were preprocessed in the Python environment. The Pandas library
in the built-in Spyder program was used to extract stress ratios of each component and
convert them into lightweight JSON format data. Subsequently, the Revit API was called in
the Revit Python Shell plugin environment. External JSON databases were read through
the interface to achieve precise alignment of component IDs between the FEM model and
the BIM physical model, as shown in Figure 16.
A three-level visual mapping system was constructed by introducing the stress change
rate (∆R). A red zone represented areas with large stress changes under foundation dis-
placement; a dark green transition zone depicted areas with significant internal force
redistribution, reflecting the dynamic conduction process of stress changes from the tower
bottom upward; a light gray stable zone used high transparency to hide information of
components less affected by stress changes. The specific judgment criteria were carried out
according to Table 5.

In the initial stage of scour, most components of the tower body are in a gray hidden

internal forces generated by slight foundation displacements are still concentrated at the

to the upper structure.
During the entire 0–60 s wind loading process, the structural dynamic response

5. Conclusions and Future Work
5.1. Conclusions
The digital twin framework proposed in this paper integrates IoT, BIM, and FEM.
Through low-level calls of the OAPI interface in the Python environment, real-time data
from the physical environment can directly drive the mechanical evolution analysis in the
virtual space. This virtual–real interaction mechanism effectively enhances the timeliness
of simulation deduction, providing innovative ideas for dynamic identification of early
damage in transmission towers. Specific conclusions are as follows:
(1) The Davenport fluctuating wind spectrum was generated based on the harmonic
superposition method, completely retaining the square term of wind load. Verified by the
Welch method, the simulated power spectrum agreed well with the theoretical target spec-
trum. Results showed that this method accurately reproduced the random characteristics
of natural wind. Meanwhile, it effectively overcame the limitations of traditional linear
assumptions, providing reliable load boundary conditions for the OAPI-driven digital
twin framework.
(2) A digital twin closed-loop mechanism driven by IoT data was successfully con-
structed. This study used the IoT monitoring network as the core information source,
relying on MQTT and OAPI interfaces to efficiently convert real-time environmental data
into dynamic inputs for the finite element analysis, driving structural wind-induced vi-
bration and scour analysis. Simultaneously, by interconnecting model nodes with on-site
IoT devices, a “virtual sensor” mapping was established. This framework ensured eh
synchronous analysis and calculation of stress and displacement responses, completing the
data closed-loop mechanism from physical to digital ends.
(3) Through nonlinear exponential spring stiffness reduction, the entire process of
dynamic degradation of transmission towers under flood scour was realistically repro-
duced. Results indicated that the structural natural vibration characteristics had a 40%
scour evolution threshold. Structural frequencies exhibited significant stage-sensitive char-
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16083620

Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 3620
22 of 25
acteristics to the degree of scour. After exceeding that threshold, the first and second
frequencies showed a rapid nonlinear decay (maximum reduction of 80.9%), with the
foundation rapidly degrading from a fixed end to a near-hinged state. Additionally, scour
induced significant load transfer effects. When scour reached 80%, the bearing capacity of
the upstream foundation basically failed; simultaneously, axial forces at downstream nodes
surged, doubling from 27 kN to 54 kN.
(4) During the entire 0–60 s loading process, the structural dynamic response exhibited
significant stage characteristics. In the initial stage of wind loading, the stress change rate
was small (0% < ∆R < 20%), the structure maintained stability, and the tower legs mainly
bore the bending moment caused by wind load. In the middle stage of wind loading, the
stress change rate further expanded (20% < ∆R < 50%), with stress spreading upward and
redistributing. At the peak moment of the wind-load time history reaching 60 s, the local
stress change rate of the tower legs reached its peak, with ∆R > 50%, and the tower bottom
became a stress concentration area, developing into a weak link in wind resistance. Finally,
the FEM mechanical response highly agreed with the damage indicator ∆R, accurately
mapping the evolution process of structural internal forces and successfully achieving
closed-loop validation of the digital twin framework.
Practically, the strong structural asymmetry observed in this study suggests that future
design and safety assessment frameworks should explicitly incorporate non-uniform scour
as a critical load case. Accounting for this spatial variability helps to ensure that localized
extreme internal forces and differential settlements are adequately addressed. Furthermore,
structural health monitoring and maintenance strategies can be optimized by targeting
these critical regions of structural degradation.
5.2. Future Work
(1) The current work primarily focused on validating the feasibility of the proposed
digital twin framework. Consequently, the analyses were conducted for wind load and
flood scour as separate, single-hazard scenarios. A comprehensive analysis of wind–flood
joint actions will be the focus of subsequent research.
(2) The current framework demonstrates the integrated feasibility of automated multi-
hazard tracking under idealized conditions, but it does not yet account for complex uncer-
tainty propagation across the independent models (e.g., wind, scour, and SSI). Following the
profound insights into probabilistic paradigms [43] and uncertainty quantification [44,45]
established by recent pioneering works, future research will focus on quantifying and
propagating these multi-source uncertainties through the digital twin framework. This
will facilitate a crucial transition from deterministic visualization to robust probabilistic
reliability assessment, thereby further perfecting the fidelity of the digital twin system.
(3) Future research will extend this framework to thermo-mechanical hazards, as
emerging risks of electric fires with thermal runaway and structural degradation are
increasingly critical trends in infrastructure resilience [46,47].
(4) While the current digital twin framework demonstrates strong numerical consis-
tency through PSD matching and validation using virtual sensors, future research will
focus on its real-world implementation. We plan to integrate physical IoT hardware (e.g.,
anemometers, sonar profilers, and strain gauges) to rigorously validate the dynamic twin-
ning accuracy under complex, real-world scour scenarios using field-measured data and
scaled laboratory experiments. Furthermore, to ensure practical scalability, future work
will explicitly evaluate the cost-benefit of different sensor deployment schemes (e.g., sparse
vs. dense networks) to optimize hardware and maintenance costs while maintaining high
monitoring reliability.
