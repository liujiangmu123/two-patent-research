A Novel Power Pylon Truss Modeler for LoD3
Reconstruction From ULS Data: Bridging the
Gap Between Visualization and Simulation
Bingtao Chang
, Xiaodong Cui
, Xiaodong Lan, Hao Xiong

Abstract—Amid growing global electricity demand and
increasingly frequent extreme weather events, ensuring the
reliable operation of power transmission infrastructure is
paramount. A major bottleneck, however, is the genera-
tion of high ﬁdelity, truss level [level of detail (LoD3)] py-
lon models suitable for engineering simulation, because
existing methods are largely limited to visual approxima-
tion and perform poorly on sparse uncrewed aerial vehicle
(UAV) light detection and ranging [UAV borne laser scan-
ning (ULS)] data. To address this critical gap, we introduce
the pylon truss modeler (PTM), a novel algorithm that, to
the best of the authors’ knowledge, enables accurate LoD3
reconstruction from challenging ULS data for the ﬁrst time.
PTM has two key innovations: a data enhancement strategy
that signiﬁcantly improves point cloud density and clarity,
and a highly efﬁcient “node-ﬁrst” reconstruction strategy.
This framework simpliﬁes reconstruction by reframing it
as a “node identiﬁcation then connection” task and fur-
ther converts 3-D truss node localization into a 1-D sig-
nal processing problem through dimensionality reduction.
Experimental validation on an 11 km transmission corri-
dor demonstrates that PTM can reconstruct complex pylon
structures with an average point-to-model RMSE (RMSEp)
of 0.137 m, a node localization RMSE of 0.098 m, and low
normalized graph edit distances of 0.048 and 0.035 for main
and diagonal members, respectively. Most importantly, we
demonstrate the use of these reconstructed models in
ﬁnite-element deformation analysis to support preliminary
structural-response assessment of in-service pylons. This
work not only validates the effectiveness of PTM, but also
establishes a novel, noncontact paradigm for transforming
real-world sensing data into mechanically analyzable pylon
models, thereby bridging the gap between visualization and
simulation.

I. INTRODUCTION
E
LECTRICAL energy is a cornerstone of modern society,
and global demand is escalating dramatically due to indus-
trial growth and the rapid advancement of artiﬁcial intelligence
[1]. Simultaneously, the transition to clean energy often widens
the geographical gap between power generation and consump-
tion, placing severe pressure on transmission grids to maintain
a stable supply [2]. This critical infrastructure is increasingly
vulnerable to extreme environmental hazards, yet its key nodes,
transmission pylons, are typically designed for normal service
loads rather than extreme conditions. As extreme weather events
become more frequent [3], there is an urgent need to assess
pylonperformanceunder extremeloads toprovideearlywarning
and prevent disasters [4]. High-ﬁdelity pylon models are an
indispensable foundation for this paradigm. They support ﬁnite
element simulations that generate such warnings [5] and enable
evaluation of whether in-service structures remain reliable and
compliant with design speciﬁcations [6]. Ultimately, model-
based solutions are essential for developing digital twins of
transmission corridors, substantially improving interoperability
and real-time responsiveness [7].
Despitethesecriticalrequirements,apersistentgapremainsin
acquiring precise 3-D models of aging transmission infrastruc-
ture [8]. More than 70% of U.S. transmission pylons that have
been in service for over 30 years lack as-built, building infor-
mation modeling -compliant models. Conventional digitization
workﬂows still rely predominantly on manual computer-aided
design (CAD) operations. However, this approach is economi-
cally prohibitive given the spatial characteristics of transmission
networks, where linear corridors can extend for hundreds of
kilometers.
Remote sensing technologies, including visible light imaging,
infrared imaging, and light detection and ranging (LiDAR), are
increasingly used in power grid operation and maintenance.
Visible light and infrared imaging are commonly employed
to detect faults in power equipment [9], [10], [11]. However,
these modalities cannot provide precise 3-D information, par-
ticularly for power pylons with complex structures. LiDAR,
owing to its capability to capture accurate 3-D coordinates, is
widely adopted for conductor modeling and clearance-zone in-
trusion monitoring [12]. Uncrewed aerial vehicle (UAV)-borne
laser scanning (ULS) further improves operational efﬁciency by

In recent years, many studies have investigated the use of ULS
point clouds for power grid digitization. For example, numerous
studies focus on classifying raw point clouds into power line,
pylon, and insulator classes [14]. These classiﬁed datasets can
then be used to verify whether transmission components comply
with speciﬁed standards [15]. However, point cloud data capture
only the instantaneous state of transmission infrastructure, en-
abling inspection at only a single moment in time [16]. To predict
future states and provide risk warnings, a crucial step is vector
reconstruction of the infrastructure [17]. For instance, Munir
et al. [18] reconstructed transmission lines as catenaries. Based
on these catenary models, the dynamic response of transmis-
sion lines under varying conditions, such as temperature [19],
wind [20], and ice accretion [21], can be simulated to assess
risks, such as line breakage or reduced clearance distance.
However, existing research has largely concentrated on mod-
eling single span lines while neglecting the pylons that connect
them [22].
This oversight reveals a core technical bottleneck: the lack
of structurally sound pylon models with correctly deﬁned truss
members prevents meaningful coupled simulation of the entire
line-pylon system [5]. Research on reconstructing power pylons
remains relatively limited, largely because of their complex con-
ﬁgurations and wide variety of types [23]. Most existing studies
produce only approximate shapes, failing to capture detailed
truss structures and the semantic attributes of their constituent
members. This limitation is fundamental: without a one-to-one
correspondence between model elements and real-world truss
members, it is impossible to assign the distinct physical proper-
ties (e.g., cross-sections and material grades) required for simu-
lation analysis. To date, only one study has reconstructed a pylon
truss structure [8], but it relied on terrestrial LiDAR (TLS) rather
than ULS data, which generally provide much higher accuracy
and point density. However, TLS data acquisition is far less
efﬁcient than ULS and is therefore impractical for reconstructing
long distance transmission corridors.
To reconstruct complex pylon truss conﬁgurations from ULS
point cloud data for engineering simulation, we propose a novel
pylon truss modeler (PTM) algorithm. The primary contribu-
tions of the proposed method are as follows.
1) To the best of the authors’ knowledge, we propose the
ﬁrst method for automatically reconstructing structurally
accurate level of detail (LoD3) pylon models from sparse
and noisy ULS point clouds. This is achieved through a
novel data enhancement workﬂow that overcomes inher-
ent data quality limitations.
2) We introduce a “node-ﬁrst” reconstruction paradigm that
signiﬁcantly improves accuracy and reduces topological
errors. This approach transforms the complex 3-D truss
node localization problem into a 1-D signal processing
problem through a dedicated dimensionality reduction
strategy.
3) We demonstrate the practical application of these
automatically reconstructed models in ﬁnite element
deformation analysis. This establishes a novel, noncon-
tact workﬂow for preliminary structural-response assess-
ment of in-service infrastructure under external loads,
thereby validating the direct utility of our models for
engineering analysis.
II. RELATED WORKS
To effectively evaluate the modeling performance of different
methods, we adapt the commonly used LoD standards from
building reconstruction and classify power-pylon models into
three categories: 1) LoD1, a simple geometric model (e.g.,
cuboids or prisms) that captures basic information, such as
position, height, and overall dimensions; 2) LoD2, a reﬁned
model that distinguishes different pylon types, including varia-
tions in the number of cross-arms or head types; and 3) LoD3,
a fully detailed and accurate truss model in which each member
corresponds one-to-one to the actual components of the real
pylon.
Based on these LoD classiﬁcations, existing power-pylon
reconstruction methods can be grouped into three general cat-
egories. Because LoD1 carries very limited information, its
reconstruction is often performed alongside pylon point-cloud
detection. For example, Awrangjeb[24] derived a pylon’s central
coordinates directly after extracting pylon points, achieving
a planar positioning accuracy of up to 0.1 m. However, this
simpliﬁed model captures only pylon location and omits height
and size information. By contrast, Shen et al. [25] used rect-
angular cross-sections to detect and describe pylons, thereby
incorporating height and size in addition to location. Although
LoD1 reconstruction is easy to process and store, it satisﬁes
only the requirements of 2-D mapping and basic power-corridor
management.
Most pylon reconstructions intended for visualization fall
into LoD2. These model-driven approaches [26] often segment
the pylon into components (e.g., head, body, and pedestal)
and register predeﬁned templates from a model library [27].
Theyemployvarioustechniques,includingMarkovchainMonte
Carlo/simulated annealing for parameter optimization [26],
[28], random sample consensus (RANSAC) for ﬁtting primary
frames [29], [30], and machine learning (support vector machine
(SVM)/deep learning (DL)) for component classiﬁcation and
matching [30], [31]. Although later studies incorporated more
detailed libraries containing primary truss structures [23], [27],
these models remain visual approximations. Their truss layouts
do not necessarily match the actual as-built conﬁguration, which
makes them unsuitable for engineering analysis. Moreover, the
high complexity and diversity of pylon types make comprehen-
sive library-based approaches difﬁcult to scale [23].
Using data constraints and truss-construction rules offers a
promising path toward LoD3 modeling. For instance, Kawasaki
and Masuda [8] used prior structural knowledge and component-
joining constraints to reconstruct pylon trusses from TLS data.
In bridge-truss reconstruction, Lamas et al. [32] applied instance
segmentation to individual truss members based on point-cloud
features, while Justo et al. [33] reconstructed each member by
extending bounding boxes and detecting collisions. Although
effective for trusses with repetitive patterns and high-accuracy
TLSdata,thesemethodsareinadequateforreconstructingpower
pylons from ULS data. The combination of low point density and
limited ranging accuracy, characteristic of ULS data, together
with the complex truss geometries of power pylons, poses sub-
stantial challenges for such data-driven approaches.

III. METHOD
In this section, we detail the proposed PTM algorithm and the
subsequent simulation analysis workﬂow. The process consists
of the following four major steps.
1)Dataenhancement:Increasingpointclouddensityandclar-
ity by leveraging the pylon’s inherent geometric characteristics.
2) Truss reconstruction: Decomposing the complex truss con-
ﬁguration into simpler components, then reconstructing them by
detecting and connecting their intersection nodes.
3) Topology restoration: Reprojecting the dimensionally re-
duced single face models into 3-D space according to their
relative geometric relationships, and correcting reconstruction
errors through graph optimization.
4) Simulation analysis: performing simulations under wind
loads on the reconstructed pylon model.
Fig. 1 illustrates the overall pipeline of the proposed algo-
rithm.
A. Data Enhancement
Power pylons typically range from 10 to 200 m in height.
The point density acquired from ULS is approximately 100–500
points per square meter. This density is insufﬁcient for capturing
truss components with steel widths of 40–250 mm. Furthermore,
the near-vertical repetition of pylon structures causes inevitable
occlusions among lower truss units, further decreasing point
density. This low-density, low-quality point cloud data presents a
critical bottleneck for truss reconstruction. Therefore, we detail a
three-step process to improve the data quality: pylon redirection,
point-density enhancement, and sharpness enhancement.
Fig. 2.
Density enhancement diagram of a pylon point cloud.
Fig. 3.
Comparison of pylon points before and after sharpening.
1) Pylon Redirection: To address the disorganized orienta-
tion of pylon point clouds, we ﬁrst identify their primary direc-
tion by principal component analysis (PCA). This direction cor-
responds to the pylon’s cross-arms, which consistently exhibit
thehighestpointdensity.Theentirepointcloudisthenreoriented
by computationally rotating this primary direction with high
density so that it is parallel to the x axis, which simpliﬁes all
subsequent processing steps.
2) Density Enhancement: Power pylons typically exhibit
highly regular and strictly symmetrical geometric conﬁgura-
tions, making them well suited for information completion based
on symmetry. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the pylon contains two
symmetric axes. To locate these axes, we ﬁrst calculate the
2-D pylon center (xc, yc) by taking the mean of the x and y
coordinates. However, because cross-arms on certain pylons
may not be symmetrical on both sides, we use only the portion
of the point cloud below the cross-arms to compute (xc, yc).
We then segment the point cloud into two or four symmetrical
parts. As shown for the ﬁrst segment in Fig. 2, each part is
subsequently ﬂipped and translated to create a rough alignment
among the segments.
The computed pylon center is often not the true geometric
center, which can cause the ﬂipping and translation operations
to align the segments inaccurately. Therefore, we employ the
iterative closest point (ICP) algorithm [34] to achieve optimal
registration.
3) Sharpness Enhancement: Because of ULS ranging er-
rors, truss points appear relatively scattered (see Fig. 3). Struc-
tural details between adjacent truss units become blurred, which
makes it difﬁcult to distinguish individual units. Therefore, we
employ bilateral ﬁltering [35] to reﬁne and sharpen the pylon
points. Traditional bilateral ﬁltering typically requires feature

B. Truss Reconstruction
Power pylons are highly intricate truss structures. Their shafts
act as vertical load bearing components, and their cross-arms
servehorizontalloadbearingfunctions.Thisdualroleposescon-
siderable challenges for reconstruction when using a single set
of rules. Consequently, decomposing the pylon into components
is essential for reducing reconstruction complexity, and this
strategy is also commonly used in model driven reconstruction
methods [23]. Moreover, the large number of truss members and
their intricate intersection relationships make direct 3-D recon-
struction computationally expensive and prone to topological
errors. Therefore, we ﬁrst reduce the problem dimensionality
to 2-D space, and then employ a reconstruction strategy that
identiﬁes intersection nodes before connecting them to form
truss members.
1) Component Decomposition: Traditional component de-
composition methods often identify high density nodes along
the vertical axis and then segment the structure at these lo-
cations [23], [27]. However, this approach is prone to over
segmentation and requires complex parameter tuning. To ad-
dress these limitations, we propose a simpler and more robust
decomposition method that leverages differences in component
symmetry.
Our method is based on a key observation: the pylon shaft
is centrally symmetric and realigns with itself after a 90◦rota-
tion, whereas the cross-arms are not centrally symmetric [see
Fig. 4(a)]. To exploit this property, the entire point cloud is ﬁrst
rotated 90◦in the horizontal plane around its 2-D center (xc, yc).
Subsequently, the ICP algorithm is used to register the rotated
cloud to the original cloud and ensure precise alignment.
Fig. 5.
Dimensionality reduction of pylon shaft. (a) 3-D point cloud.
(b) 2-D point cloud. (c) Truss unit types.
After this transformation, we calculate the distance dr from
each original point to its nearest neighbor in the rotated cloud
[see Fig. 4(b)]. This distance serves as an effective symmetry
metric. Points are classiﬁed as belonging to the pylon shaft (Ns)
if dr is below a threshold Sdth; otherwise, they are classiﬁed
as cross-arms. Although this initial process separates the shaft
from the arms, all cross-arms are merged into a single segment
[see Fig. 4(c)]. To isolate them individually, we perform in-
stance segmentation by applying a connected component label-
ing algorithm [36]. In the subsequent reconstruction phase, each
cross-arm segment and the pylon shaft are treated as independent
structural units.
2) Component Reconstruction: This section details the truss
reconstruction process, using the pylon shaft as a representative
example. Mathematically, the proposed dimensionality reduc-
tion is deﬁned as a two stage mapping process that transforms
node localization from a spatial search in R3 into a linear search
in R1. First (3-D →2-D), by leveraging the shaft’s central sym-
metry, we project the entire 3-D point cloud P = {(xi, yi, zi)}
onto the 2-D xoz plane [see Fig. 5(b)]. The mapping is de-
ﬁned as f3→2 : (xi, yi, zi) →(xi, zi). This projection removes
redundant depth information along the y-axis while preserving
truss proﬁle boundaries required for structural analysis. Second
(2-D →1-D), within the 2-D domain, the search space is further
constrained by the reconstructed main members. Let the ﬁtted
main member be represented by the linear equation L(z) :
x = kz + b. The search space for diagonal nodes is strictly
constrained to the region Ω = {(x, z) ∈R2 : |x −L(z)| ≤τ},
where τ represents the buffer threshold, set to 0.1 m in this
study, and determined by point cloud thickness. This constraint
reduces the degrees of freedom from two variables, (x, z), to
one variable, (z), allowing us to map point density within Ω to a
1-D signal array S(z) for efﬁcient peak detection. This targeted
reduction strategy minimizes degrees of freedom in the solution
domain and substantially streamlines the overall process.

Once nodes on both sides are identiﬁed, diagonal trusses
can be reconstructed based on point cloud distribution between
adjacent nodes. Each diagonal node can connect only to its three
nearest nodes on the opposite side. As shown in Fig. 8, each
prospective connection is ﬁrst divided into 0.1 m segments. We
then count how many segments contain at least one point, and
compute connection probability as the ratio of segments with
points to the total number of segments. If this ratio exceeds 0.5,
the connection is considered valid. This strategy reliably avoids
false negatives, which are missed diagonals caused by point
cloud sparsity, and false positives, which are spurious diagonals
caused by noise.
Reconstruction of auxiliary members follows a similar 1-D
search method. Under the combined constraints of main and di-
agonal members, auxiliary nodes are also identiﬁed by detecting
peaks in the 1-D sequence using AMPD.
C. Topology Restoration
1) 3-D Restoration: The reconstructed truss models initially
exist in 2-D. To restore these models to 3-D space, we leverage
the shared edge property between adjacent faces (for example,
the shared edge is line PaPbPc in Fig. 9(b), which connects the
front and left faces). Speciﬁcally, the projection of the left face

from any node on the left face to the xmin plane equals the
distance from its horizontal projection on PaPbPc to xmin. Like-
wise, because of the pylon’s central symmetry, the horizontal
distance from any node on the front face to the ymin plane equals
the distance from its horizontal projection on PaPbPc to xmin.

D. Simulation Analysis
With the acquisition of a detailed LoD3 truss model enriched
with component properties, the reconstructed pylon can be fur-
ther used for structural simulation rather than only geometric
visualization. This capability provides a direct link between
ULS-based reconstruction and engineering analysis, allowing
the reconstructed model to be converted into a mechanically
analyzable ﬁnite element model for deformation assessment
under external loads.
In this study, we demonstrate this capability through a static
wind-induced deformation analysis. The PTM-generated truss
model was converted into a line-element ﬁnite element model,
where each reconstructed truss member was represented by a
beam element and the reconstructed nodes were used as element
endpoints. Adjacent or coincident endpoints belonging to the
same structural joint were merged to ensure model connectivity.
The material and cross-sectional properties were speciﬁed from
a predeﬁned structural material and section library according to
the actual conﬁguration of the reconstructed pylon. All pylon
members were assigned Q355 low-alloy high-strength struc-
tural steel, with an elastic modulus of E = 2.06 × 105 MPa,
Poisson’s ratio of ν = 0.30, density of ρ = 7850 kg/m3, and
yield strength of fy = 355 MPa. Based on the structural roles
of different member categories, the main members, diagonal
members, and auxiliary members were assigned L220 × 22,
L140 × 12, and L90 × 8 angle-steel sections, respectively.
The static load case considered in this simulation consisted of
self-weight, conductor tension, and wind load. The self-weight
was calculated from the assigned material density and section
properties. The conductor tension was applied as equivalent
concentrated forces at the corresponding cross-arm attachment
points. For the wind-load simulation, the basic wind pressure
was set to w0 = 0.35 kN/m2. The wind pressure acting on each
pylon member was calculated according to its projected area and
orientation, and then converted into equivalent line loads in the
ﬁnite element model.
Fixed-support boundary conditions were applied at the pylon-
foot nodes by constraining all translational and rotational de-
greesoffreedom.Sincethepresentsimulationwasastaticlinear-
elastic analysis, damping was not included. The reconstructed
truss members were discretized into beam elements with a mesh
resolutionof0.1 m.Inotherwords,themaximumelementlength
was set to 0.1 m during mesh generation.
IV. EXPERIMENTS AND ANALYSIS
A. Experiment Data
The PTM algorithm was validated on a dataset gathered
from an 11 km power transmission corridor, using a LiAir X3
LiDAR-equipped UAV. The dataset contains 30 power pylons
with varying types and truss structures, speciﬁcally 8 tension
pylons and 22 suspension pylons. The average point density is
152.6 pts/m2. Fig. 11 provides point cloud examples of four
representative pylons, each featuring distinct truss conﬁgura-
tions. These variations offer a robust test for evaluating the PTM
algorithm.
In the study area, mountainous terrain and dense vegetation
caused signiﬁcant occlusion around the pylon pedestals [as
depicted in Fig. 11(b) and (d)], resulting in disorganized point
data in these regions. To resolve this, the pedestal sections were
cropped out, leaving only the pylon shaft and cross-arms for
further processing.
B. Parameter Settings
The main PTM parameters were set as follows. In bilateral
ﬁltering, the spatial and feature kernel widths were σs = 0.30 m
and σf = 0.20 m, and the inlier buffer width for candidate
member evaluation was τ = 0.10 m; these values were ﬁxed
for all pylons. The AMPD peak selection scale Ss was chosen
from {4, 5, 6, 7} according to the 1-D inlier-count sequence:
Ss = 4 for pylons #2, #3, #5, #6, #10, #11, #20, #21, #25,
and #30; Ss = 5 for #1, #8, #12, #13, #15, #16, #23, #27, and
#29; Ss = 6 for #4, #7, #9, #14, #18, #19, #24, and #28; and
Ss = 7 for #17, #22, and #26. Smaller Ss values preserve local
peak variations, whereas larger values suppress ﬂuctuations in
smoother node-spacing patterns.

V. DISCUSSION
A. Contribution of Data Enhancement
To quantify each data enhancement step’s contribution to
reconstruction accuracy, we conducted ablation experiments on
point-density enhancement and sharpening individually. Fig. 17
shows that only the complete data enhancement pipeline pro-
duces a near-perfect pylon model (see Fig. 17). By contrast,
omitting point-density enhancement [see Fig. 17(c) and (d)]
introduces severe distortion in the upper portion of the main
shaft, likely due to sparse point clouds caused by steep vertical
angles of the main members. In addition, the lack of point-
density enhancement signiﬁcantly increases missed detections
of diagonal and auxiliary members. Omitting sharpening [see
Fig. 17(b)] can also mislead the AMPD algorithm in heav-
ily populated areas (blue circle), presumably because blurred
point-cloud boundaries prevent accurate truss-member identi-
ﬁcation. Moreover, as shown in the red circle, sharpening im-
proves the detection of auxiliary members with dispersed point

B. Advantages of Reconstructing Nodes Over Lines
While existing methods have employed RANSAC line ﬁt-
ting for steel structure reconstruction, including partial diago-
nal member detection in power pylons, this approach exhibits
critical limitations when processing densely arranged auxiliary
members. To compare the performance of PTM against this tra-
ditional strategy, we reconstructed pylon #2 using both methods.
The results demonstrate (see Fig. 18) that PTM’s node-based
method produces a more complete and topologically correct
reconstruction of auxiliary members, whereas the line-based
RANSAC result is fragmented and inaccurate. This performance
gap stems from a fundamental difference in parametric com-
plexity. The line-based ﬁtting must estimate six parameters for
each linear element, whereas our node-based approach requires
only two parameters per component. This threefold increase
in dimensionality exponentially expands the solution space for
line-based methods, making them highly susceptible to noise
and leading to erroneous results in areas with sparse data [red
circle, Fig. 18(b)].
C. Implications for Industrial Informatics and Digital
Twins
This work represents more than a methodological advance-
ment; it establishes a foundational data-to-model pipeline for
industrial informatics. The PTM algorithm addresses a core
challenge for Digital Twins: the automated generation of high-
ﬁdelity, simulation-ready models from real-world sensor data.
This automated pipeline provides the essential geometric and
semantic foundation for a valid cyber-physical representation,
moving beyond a mere visual replica. Furthermore, it enables
a shift from reactive to data-driven predictive maintenance in
smart grids. Our successful application of these models in ﬁnite
element deformation analysis serves as a proof-of-concept for a
novel, noncontact workﬂow, transforming raw LiDAR data into
actionable structural-response information.
D. Limitations and Future Work
The applicability of PTM is mainly limited by its geometric
and topological assumptions. The symmetry-based data en-
hancement strategy may introduce misalignment for asymmet-
ric or damaged pylons, such as unequal-leg pylons on steep
terrain, which can weaken the 1-D density signal and affect
peak detection. In addition, the current decomposition strategy
assumes simply connected sub-components and is less suitable
for nonstandard topologies, such as O-shaped or wine-glass
pylons, with closed-loop heads. Future work will explore a
boundary-ﬁrst reconstruction paradigm, in which continuous
3-D boundary lines or main members are ﬁrst extracted as
global constraints for detecting diagonal and auxiliary members.
Conﬁdence attributes may also be assigned to reconstructed
members to indicate reliability under asymmetry or damage.
Data acquisition and simulation settings also impose lim-
itations. PTM does not yet fully reconstruct pylon pedestals
because vegetation occlusion often causes sparse lower region
point clouds; denser ﬂy-around UAV surveys and improved
classiﬁcation could mitigate this issue. The current simulation
further demonstrates only static linear-elastic deformation un-
der a representative wind-load case. Future work will extend
the framework to coupled pylon-line and dynamic analyses,
including modal response, wind-induced vibration, conduc-
tor galloping, seismic response, icing, and high-temperature
load cases, supported by additional mechanical parameters and
sensor-based model updating.
VI. CONCLUSION
This study introduced the PTM, a novel algorithm designed
to reconstruct structurally accurate, LoD3 power pylon models
directly from sparse and noisy ULS data. Addressing the crit-
ical gap between visualization-oriented models and the high-
ﬁdelity requirements of engineering analysis, the PTM algo-
rithm successfully overcomes the inherent limitations of ULS
data through an innovative pipeline. Key contributions include
a data enhancement strategy and a “node-ﬁrst” reconstruction
approach. Validation on a comprehensive dataset from an 11 km
transmission corridor, encompassing 30 pylons of varying de-
signs, demonstrated the algorithm’s robustness, achieving an
average point-to-model RMSE (RMSEp) of 0.137 m, a node
localization RMSE of 0.098 m, and low normalized graph edit
distances of 0.048 and 0.035 for main and diagonal members, re-
spectively. More signiﬁcantly, this research demonstrates the di-
rect application of reconstructed pylon models in ﬁnite-element
deformation analysis. By successfully performing ﬁnite element
analysis on the generated models to assess structural defor-
mation under wind loads, we have bridged the long-standing
divide between remote sensing data acquisition and practical
structural assessment. This work not only validates the utility of
our models but also establishes a novel, noncontact workﬂow for
preliminary structural-response assessment of in-service power
infrastructure, paving the way for the development of digital
twins for power transmission corridors.
